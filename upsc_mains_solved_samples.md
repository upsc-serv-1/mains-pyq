## Question 1 (Year: 2023)

**What was the difference between Mahatma Gandhi and Rabindranath Tagore in their approach towards education and nationalism? (10 Marks, 150 Words)**

### Answer

### Introduction

Approach of Gandhi and Tagore towards education and nationalism were unique and significant in nature.

### Body

**Gandhi's Outlook:**
- **On Education (Wardha Scheme - 1937):** Basic education and mass-based education focusing on vernaculars and value-based learning.
- **Focus on 3H:** Harmonious development of mind, body, and heart focusing on Hand (Skills/Job-Centric Approach/Productive Crafts), Heart (Moral-Purification), and Head (Spiritual-Upliftment).
- **On Nationalism:** Expressed in the form of Swaraj and Ramrajya, focusing on a secular spirit and religious universalism.
- **Self-Generated Progression:** Growth of nationalism in India was a result of cultural diversity, moral and spiritual progression, independent of British rule.

**Tagore's Outlook:**
- **On Education:** A dynamic, unique approach that rejected the conventional method of schooling in favor of a nature and practice-based learning curriculum with a focus on analysis, interpretation, and moral values.
- **Institutional Expression:** Expressed in institutions like Shanti Niketan (1863) and Vishwa Bharati (1921), promoting environmental consciousness, interpersonal skills, and community-oriented awareness.
- **On Nationalism:** Visualized nationalism beyond the national map and borders, focusing on international humanistic concerns, promoting equality, and ending discrimination and privilege systems.

### Conclusion

Due to its rich cultural diversity and economic prosperity, Indians also made significant progress in the fields of technology, literature, science, and architecture etc.

## Question 2 (Year: 2023)

**How did the colonial rule affect the tribals in India and what was the tribal response to the colonial oppression? (10 Marks, 150 Words)**

### Answer

### Introduction

Tribals occupied the forest areas and the resource richness of these areas attracted the British to these areas for their commercial potential. The British policies thus led to unwanted interference in tribal societies.

### Body

**Impact of Colonial Policies on Tribals:**
- **Land Revenue Policies:** Implementation of land revenue systems like Zamindari led to forced commercialization and exploitation in tribal regions, such as those of the Chaur and Munda tribes.
- **Policy of Interference:** Intervention in social and religious practices, such as banning the Mariah sacrifice among the Khond tribe, alongside cultural disruption by Christian missionaries.
- **Expansionist Encroachments:** Tribal communities faced land dispossession and encroachments by colonial powers and landlords, as seen in the Santhal regions.
- **Restriction of Forest Rights:** Forest Acts of 1865 and 1878 restricted traditional tribal access to forests, hunting, gathering, and shifting (jhum) cultivation, affecting tribes like the Chenchus.
- **Commercialization of Forests:** Oak and timber extraction for railways/navy displaced tribes and opened forest lands to exploitative moneylenders.
- **Criminalization of Tribes:** Banning traditional long-distance trade by designating certain tribes as criminal under the Criminal Tribes Act.

**Response of Tribals towards Colonial Rule:**
- **Resistance and Rebellion:** Armed uprisings against colonial forces, such as the Santhal Rebellion (1855-1856) and the Bastar Rebellion (1910).
- **Isolation and Avoidance:** Some tribal groups (especially in the Himalayan regions) withdrew to remote areas to preserve their culture and autonomy.
- **Cultural Preservation:** Leaders like Birsa Munda urged their tribes to clean villages, give up alcohol, reject belief in witchcraft, and protect their heritage.
- **Non-Violent Opposition:** Opting for civil disobedience, such as rent refusal in the Bhagat movement and launching the Forest Satyagraha by the Chenchu tribe.

### Conclusion

Rise of colonial interference in the tribal regions across India gave rise to the anti-British sentiments during the 19th and early 20th century. This helped the nationalist leaders to unite these tribal regions after Independence following the policy of non-interference and inclusive development.

## Question 3 (Year: 2022)

**Why did the armies of the British East India Company – mostly comprising of Indian soldiers – win consistently against the more numerous and better equipped armies of the then Indian rulers? Give reasons. (10 Marks, 150 Words)**

### Answer

### Introduction

The British East India Company (EIC) defeated larger Indian armies through military and political superiority. Despite comprising mostly Indian sepoys, the EIC armies triumphed due to advanced tactics, discipline and leadership.

### Body

**Reasons Behind the Victory of East India Company:**
- **Unified Command:** The EIC maintained a unified, centralized, and highly organized command structure over their armies.
- **Latest Technology:** Armies were equipped with the latest weapon technology, supported by the most advanced naval forces of the era.
- **Single Objective:** The EIC operated with a single-minded goal to protect and expand British trade and economic interests.
- **External Support and Leadership:** EIC military campaigns received strong support from the British Parliament and Crown, guided by capable leaders like Robert Clive, Warren Hastings, and Lord Cornwallis.
- **Strategic Policies:** Deployment of strategic frameworks like the Subsidiary Alliance and the Ring of Fence to systematically isolate and displace Indian rulers.
- **Permanent Standing Army:** The EIC maintained a permanent, professional standing army, whereas Indian rulers relied on soldiers who returned to farming during peace times.

### Conclusion

Other reasons which helped the British included in-fighting amongst the Indian rulers, good network of espionage by the EIC and betrayal of Indian rulers by locals (e.g. Ghasiti Begum or Omi Chand in Battle of Plassey).

## Question 4 (Year: 2022)

**Why was there a sudden spurt in famines in colonial India since the mid-eighteenth century? Give reasons. (15 Marks, 250 Words)**

### Answer

### Introduction

Over dependence on monsoon coupled with British policy failures has caused many famines since the middle of 18th century e.g., Bengal Famines (1770), the great famine (1876).

### Body

**Reasons Behind Sudden Spurt in Famines:**
- **Commercialization of Agriculture:** Forced cultivation of cash crops like indigo and poppy, particularly under the Dual Government model in Bengal, left farmers with no food reserves during crop failures.
- **Exploitative Land Revenue Systems:** Heavy taxation under systems like Zamindari and Ryotwari drained agrarian profits, leaving farmers with no surplus wealth to survive droughts.
- **Lack of Agricultural Investment:** Negligible state spending on irrigation infrastructure, such as canal networks, left agriculture highly vulnerable to droughts.
- **Export of Food Grain:** Consistent export of surplus food grains to other nations to maintain open trade policies, prioritizing imperial profits over domestic food security.
- **Restrictions on Grain Storage:** Regulations preventing farmers from storing surplus grain under the suspicion of tax evasion.
- **Lack of Disaster Management:** Absence of systematic famine relief measures, tax suspensions, or state-run food storage policies to mitigate disasters.

### Conclusion

Such policies weakened the self-sufficiency of rural India, gave rise of extreme poverty and hunger and weakened the Indian economy.

## Question 5 (Year: 2023)

**What are the main features of Vedic society and religion? Do you think some of the features are still prevailing in Indian society? (15 Marks, 250 Words)**

### Answer

### Introduction

Though Indo-Aryans were a sub-branch of Aryan family, they developed in India—a unique culture—compared to European and Iranian Aryans.

### Body

**Main Features of Vedic Society:**
- **Kinship and Family:** The patriarchal family unit (Kula) formed the basis of society, with texts emphasizing a preference for sons (Praja).
- **Social Stratification:** Dominance of the priestly (Brahmins) and ruling (Kshatriyas) classes, institutionalized by the Chaturvarna system, which restricted the rights of women and Shudras.
- **Educational Access:** The Dwija (twice-born) tradition restricted Upanayana (sacred thread ceremony) and Vedic education to the three upper castes.
- **Rigidity of Caste System:** Early occupational mobility was high, but later Vedic times saw caste groupings become hereditary, reducing social mobility.

**Main Features of Vedic Religion:**
- **Nature Worship:** Deification of natural forces like rain (Indra), fire (Agni), and water (Varuna) through prayers and yagyas.
- **Materialistic Goals:** Rituals were oriented toward acquiring cattle wealth (Pashu), sons, and health rather than salvation (moksha).
- **Later Vedic Developments:** Proliferation of complex rituals, deification of gods like Prajapati, Vishnu, and Rudra, and the emergence of philosophical counter-movements in the Upanishads.

**Elements of Continuity in Modern India:**
- **Family Structure:** The family remains the primary unit of social organization, heavily oriented around kinship.
- **Patriarchy:** Patriarchal norms continue to influence social dynamics and family decisions.
- **Varna/Caste System:** The hierarchical structure of caste continues to persist in social and marital relations.
- **Religious Practices:** Ritual prayers, yagyas, nature reverence, and devotion to deities like Vishnu and Rudra (Shiva) remain central.
- **Philosophical Base:** Upanishadic philosophy continues to serve as the bedrock of Indian philosophical thought.

### Conclusion

The continuity in the features of Vedic society and religion up to even our times almost makes it a living cultural heritage. But it is also desired that the positive elements are supported and negative ones, like secondary position given to women and depressed classes, are weeded out.
